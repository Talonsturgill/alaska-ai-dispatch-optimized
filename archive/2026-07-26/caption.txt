Alaska told about 3,500 people the state had information they may not be citizens. Then 3,048 of them went inactive on the voter roll.

Elections Director Carol Beecher has said a normal quarterly pass comes back with more like 200 names. In March the division sent the DMV a different kind of request, covering roughly 15,200 license and ID applicants. Those two figures are not the same unit, one is names returned and one is applicants sent, so nobody should be dividing them. The DMV's own July 23 memo called the March request non-routine.

Citizens are in that group. Rep. Andrew Gray of Anchorage told the division the people reaching his office are actual citizens receiving the notice. Chris Waigl of Fairbanks got her Alaska license in 2019 and naturalized in 2025. She was listed inactive as a non citizen until she sent in her naturalization certificate and the division corrected the record.

Neither record was wrong. Her 2019 file was accurate in 2019 and her 2025 certificate was accurate in 2025. Beecher named the mechanism herself, that some of this is probably very old data where people never went back to tell the DMV they had naturalized, and that these agencies do not necessarily talk to each other.

Rep. Kevin McCabe of Big Lake is right that this is the division's actual job, and the guardrails held. Nobody was removed from the rolls, nobody lost the right to vote, an inactive Alaskan can still cast a questioned ballot, and Beecher says no documentation is required to clear it. The cost landed somewhere else. Some residents who tried to call reached full voicemail boxes.

No source in this story names AI, machine learning, or an algorithm anywhere in the process, which is the whole reason it is instructive. Automated screening gets sold on the first cost. The 3,048 records that had to be answered one at a time are the second cost, and no procurement has a line item for that.

Who should carry the cost of a wrong flag, the agency that widened the query or the citizen who has to prove a stale record stale?
